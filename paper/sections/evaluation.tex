\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Research questions}

The corpus and measurement pipeline answer four questions, each
falsifiable against the labeled data:
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] Per detector, what are precision, recall, and
    F\textsubscript{1} on samples the detectors were tuned against?
  \item[RQ2] Do the detectors generalize to held-out samples they were
    not tuned against?
  \item[RQ3] Do two independent engines agree byte-for-byte?
  \item[RQ4] Where does the measurement fall short?
\end{description}
Every quantity below traces to
\texttt{docs/evaluation.md}~\cite{primitivehub:2026:eval} and the JSON
baselines under \texttt{bench/baseline/}; the authoring
discipline~\cite{primitivehub:2026:paper-readme} forbids any number not
produced by that pipeline.

\subsection{Corpus}

The Adversarial Corpus v0.2 ships 124 labeled samples: 99 in-sample,
used during detector iteration, and 25 held out. The in-sample slice is
52 adversarial, 37 benign, and 10 borderline (where reasonable labelers
could mark either way). The held-out slice is 25 adversarial samples;
it carries no benign samples by design, because its purpose is to detect
overfit recall, not overfit precision. Per-detector in-sample support
ranges from roughly 30 (CCI-002) to 45 (CCI-007, CCI-009). Of the 77
adversarial samples across both slices, 66 are marked
\texttt{incident\_reference: synthesized}, composed to exercise a
specific shape, and 11 carry real-incident
provenance~\cite{primitivehub:2026:corpus-spec}. Each sample records a
primary CCI category, an expected verdict in \{PASS, WARN, FAIL\}, and a
provenance note. Authoring and review are by repository maintainers; the
corpus is append-only, and removing a sample requires an ADR-level
justification.

\subsection{Scoring}

Per sample, we score the \emph{primary} detector, the one whose category
matches the sample's label. An adversarial or borderline sample scores a
true positive if the verdict severity meets or exceeds the expected
severity, and a false negative otherwise. A benign sample scores a false
positive if any detector raises a verdict at or above WARN, and a true
negative if it stays silent. Cross-trip firing (detector $X$ on detector
$Y$'s adversarial content) collapses into the false-positive/true-negative
dimension only, since there is no ground truth for $X$ on $Y$-family
attacks. Per-detector precision, recall, and F\textsubscript{1} use the
standard formulas~\cite{Powers:2011:evaluation-precision-recall};
macro-averaging is unweighted, because each category is a separate
defense rather than a frequency class.

The held-out slice is measured and reported separately, and the
regression gate does not enforce it~\cite{primitivehub:2026:adr-0038}.
That is deliberate: a gate that optimized the held-out numbers would
turn the overfit probe into another training set. The parity check
(\texttt{bench/scripts/parity\_check.py}) compares per-detector tuples
across engines and exits non-zero on drift.

\subsection{Reading the gap}

The contrast between the two tables is the paper's most useful result.
The in-sample gate guarantees that the engines agree on every sample
they have seen, and they do. The held-out slice removes that guarantee
and asks a harder question: when a new attack arrives, do both engines
catch it? For the Python engine the answer is yes across all 25; for the
TypeScript port it is no for seven. The misses concentrate in the
detectors with the most held-out support (CCI-005 and CCI-006), which is
where a port is most likely to lag the canonical engine on shapes it was
never tuned against. These are open findings, not closed claims. Each is
a candidate for a lockstep ratchet (Path C) recorded in the
false-negative ledger, and each will move the TypeScript engine toward
the Python engine's held-out recovery in a later round.

The combined reading of RQ1 and RQ2 is therefore: the detectors
are saturated on the in-sample slice at this corpus size, with one
known precision item; and they generalize fully in one engine but
partially in the other. A single all-1.000 figure would have hidden the
one result a reviewer should most want to see.

\subsection{Statistical confidence}
\label{subsec:eval-ci}

A point estimate on a small slice can mislead, so we report Wilson score
95\% confidence intervals on the underlying proportions. These are
computed in closed form from the committed
\texttt{(tp, fp, tn, fn)} counts, so they are reproducible without
re-running the engines. Table~\ref{tab:ci} gives the load-bearing
quantities.

Two readings follow. First, the intervals are honest about size: the
single CCI-005 false positive yields a precision point of 0.833 but a
95\% interval from 0.436 to 0.970, so we cannot yet claim a tight bound
on that detector's precision. Second, the cross-engine gap is suggestive
but not air-tight: the Python held-out interval ($[0.867, 1.000]$) sits
almost entirely above the TypeScript point estimate (0.720), yet the two
intervals overlap, so the gap is best described as a measured drift to
close, not a settled performance difference. Both readings argue the
same way the limitations below do: the result needs a larger corpus
before any interval narrows enough to support a general claim.

\subsection{Performance}
\label{subsec:eval-perf}

Static validation is only useful in CI if it is cheap. We timed the full
ten-detector Python pass over all 124 corpus primitives (mean size
1{,}424\,B, median 458\,B), five iterations after warmup, single-threaded
on a commodity host. Per-primitive latency for the complete pass is a
median of 1.5\,ms, a 95th percentile of 4.7\,ms, and a 99th percentile of
20.6\,ms; the tail is driven by the deliberately oversized
context-bloat sample (101\,KB). Throughput is roughly 227 primitives per
second per core. The cost is dominated by linear regex scans and a
single markdown-context pass, so it scales with primitive size rather
than repository size, and a per-file scan adds negligible overhead to a
pull request. We report the Python engine here; an equivalent TypeScript
measurement and a size-scaling curve are deferred to the next round.

\subsection{Ablation and a naive baseline}
\label{subsec:eval-ablation}

To isolate what the context-awareness machinery contributes, we
re-measured the in-sample slice with each analyzer neutralized, reusing
the same scoring as the regression gate (the \texttt{full} row
reproduces the committed baseline, which validates the harness).
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the result. The \texttt{naive} row, with
both analyzers off, is the detectors' raw pattern pools with no context
awareness: a naive keyword/regex baseline.

\bottomrule

Two findings, one expected and one not. The semantic-context analyzer is
load-bearing: removing the negation, mention, declaration, and
quoted-phrase predicates raises false positives from one to five and
drops macro precision from 0.983 to 0.931. The mechanism is the S-013
case (Section~\ref{sec:case-studies}): without negation detection, the
benign ``what NOT to do'' primitive that names \texttt{curl|bash} is
flagged. Against the naive pattern pool, then, the full pipeline cuts
false positives fivefold on this corpus. The unexpected finding is that
removing the markdown-context analyzer changes nothing measurable on the
present in-sample slice. Historically it closed three precision-defense
ledger entries, but the current in-sample benign set does not exercise
the fenced-code and frontmatter paths it guards, so its measured
contribution here is zero. We report that rather than assume the
component helps; it also marks a concrete corpus gap, namely benign
samples that stress code-block handling. These are internal ablations
against an internal naive baseline; external baselines (Semgrep- or
YARA-style rules, an LLM-as-judge classifier, and a human-review study)
remain the larger comparative work, discussed below.

\subsection{Limitations (RQ4)}
\label{subsec:eval-limitations}

We enumerate the limitations rather than imply there are none.

\paragraph{Corpus size} v0.2 ships 124 samples with per-detector support
of 30 to 45. F\textsubscript{1} on that support is the best
demonstration available at this size; it is not the same claim as
F\textsubscript{1} on tens of thousands of samples. Tenfold expansion is
the next-round work.

\paragraph{Cross-engine recall gap} The TypeScript engine misses seven
held-out attacks the Python engine catches. Until that closes, the
agent-side gate (which runs the TypeScript engine) carries weaker
held-out recall than the server-side pipeline. This is the most
material limitation, and we surface it in the headline rather than a
footnote.

\paragraph{Uneven held-out support} Held-out support ranges from one
sample (CCI-002, CCI-008, CCI-009, CCI-010) to seven (CCI-005). A
single held-out sample is a weak generalization probe. Matching the
in-sample density is the v0.3 corpus target.

\paragraph{Static detection only} The corpus measures detectors on
primitive content. It does not measure runtime-injection defense or
training-time poisoning~\cite{Carlini:2024:webcrawl-poisoning}; those map
to other layers of the Trust Runtime.

\paragraph{English-language bias} Adversarial samples are written
primarily in English. Attacker phrasing in Russian, Chinese, Arabic, and
Spanish is uncovered, and multilingual expansion is deferred to v0.3.

\paragraph{Vendor-list scope} The confusables detector defends the 35
single-word vendors in its list. The full UTS~\#39 confusables
distribution and UAX~\#31 identifier rules are the natural extension once
the list outgrows manual curation.

\paragraph{Synthesized provenance} Most adversarial samples (66 of 77)
are synthesized. Field-incident provenance grows as the \texttt{/check}
route accumulates real reports.

\paragraph{Single-team verification} The same team wrote the detectors
and the labels. The corpus and gate are open and reproducible by design;
independent replication is invited through the disclosure surface in
\texttt{SECURITY.md}~\cite{primitivehub:2026:security}, and ledger
closures credit external reporters. We do not yet report an inter-rater
agreement statistic, because labeling has so far been single-team; a
second independent labeling pass with a Cohen's $\kappa$ is the planned
mitigation, not merely a disclosed gap.

\paragraph{Only an internal baseline so far} Section~\ref{subsec:eval-ablation}
reports an analyzer ablation and a naive pattern-pool baseline, which
isolate the semantic-context analyzer's contribution. It does not yet
report \emph{external} baselines: a generic-rule comparison (Semgrep- or
YARA-style), an LLM-as-judge classifier (the obvious ``why not just use
an LLM?'' question), or a human-review study (most relevant for the
invisible-Unicode and encoded-payload cases). Those comparisons, against
a larger corpus, are the central evaluation work of the next round.
Accordingly, the present evaluation should be read as
detector-versus-corpus and detector-versus-naive, not as a comparison
with state-of-practice tools.

\subsection{Threats to validity}

\paragraph{Internal} One team authored detectors and labels. The
mitigation is the append-only false-negative ledger: every
prediction-to-closure chain is auditable, and a reviewer can re-run any
closure's measurement to check its F\textsubscript{1} delta.

\paragraph{External} The corpus is mostly synthesized; real content will
surface shapes outside v0.2. The mitigation is structural: a
field-reported miss becomes a corpus sample, then a ledger entry, then a
lockstep ratchet, without re-architecting the pipeline.

\paragraph{Construct} F\textsubscript{1} measures agreement between a
verdict and a manifest that encodes maintainers' beliefs about what
should fire. The borderline label and the four-path triage make that
disagreement recoverable rather than silent: relabel a sample (Path B)
or ratchet a detector (Path C), but never quietly accept.
