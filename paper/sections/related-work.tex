\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Software supply-chain provenance}

The last decade of software-supply-chain work focused on the
build pipeline. SLSA~\cite{SLSA:2023:provenance-spec} formalises
build-provenance levels; in-toto~\cite{in-toto:2016:supply-chain}
provides the predicate-based attestation framework PrimitiveHub
inherits; Sigstore~\cite{Sigstore:2021:keyless-signing} provides
keyless signing via OIDC for the public-registry path. The
common thread is verifying that a compiled artifact originated
from a specific source commit and was signed by an authorized
identity. PrimitiveHub extends the chain by adding a semantic-
validation layer that runs \emph{before} the signing step:
content the chain would otherwise validate as authentic-but-
unsafe is rejected at registry-publish time.

The chain of supply-chain incidents that motivates this
extension is well-cataloged. SolarWinds in
2020~\cite{SolarWinds:2020:supply-chain}, Codecov in
2021~\cite{Codecov:2021:bash-uploader},
\verb|ua-parser-js|~\cite{ua-parser-js:2021:npm-typosquat} the
same year, \verb|eslint-config-prettier| in
2024~\cite{eslint-config-prettier:2024:maintainer-compromise},
\verb|xz-utils| (CVE-2024-3094) the same
year~\cite{xz-utils:2024:CVE-2024-3094}, and the September 2025
Shai-Hulud npm worm~\cite{Shai-Hulud:2025:npm-worm}. Each one
either exploited or motivated a specific defense layer in the
SLSA / Sigstore / in-toto stack. PrimitiveHub's Top 10 names
the analogous defense layer for agent-context primitives.

\subsection{Prompt-injection defense}

Prior work on prompt-injection defense has focused on runtime
mitigations. Greshake et al.~\cite{Greshake:2023:prompt-injection-survey}
catalog the indirect-prompt-injection attack surface and discuss
runtime-side defenses: input filtering, retrieval sanitization,
output post-processing, and instruction-hierarchy training.
Wallace et al.~\cite{Wallace:2024:instruction-hierarchy} formalise
training the LLM itself to recognise privileged versus
non-privileged instruction sources at inference time. Both lines
operate at agent execution time; both leave the cost of a
detected attack (credentials lost, side effects committed)
partly paid before the defense fires.

PrimitiveHub takes a different position. Its detectors run at
registry-publish time; verification runs at agent-load time.
The artifact never reaches the consuming agent unless it passed
the Top 10. The discipline shift is the same one package
managers made between ``warn on install'' and ``block install
of known-bad'' --- pulling the defense earlier in the pipeline.
The two lines are complementary: a runtime-side defense will
catch attacks our static pass missed; our static pass keeps the
runtime-side defense's workload manageable by blocking the
known-bad shapes before they reach the model.

\subsection{OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications}

OWASP's Top 10 for LLM Applications
2025~\cite{OWASP:2025:llm-top10} defines a ten-category
taxonomy spanning prompt injection (LLM01), insecure output
handling (LLM02), training-data poisoning (LLM03), model denial
of service (LLM04), supply-chain vulnerabilities (LLM05), and
five others covering sensitive-information disclosure, insecure
plugin design, excessive agency, overreliance, and model theft.
PrimitiveHub's Top 10 overlaps the OWASP catalog at the
LLM01 (prompt injection) and LLM05 (supply chain) categories;
the crosswalk in \texttt{docs/owasp-llm-top10-crosswalk.md}
documents the alignment row-by-row.

The scope boundary is explicit. OWASP's runtime categories
(LLM02 insecure output handling, LLM04 denial of service, LLM06
post-inference disclosure) require defense layers a static
content scan cannot provide; those layers are typically
provider-side or proxy-side, not registry-side. PrimitiveHub
covers the LLM01 + LLM05 slice that is detectable statically on
primitive content. The crosswalk explicitly disclaims the
runtime layers as out of scope rather than claiming coverage.

\subsection{Antivirus industry methodology}

The antivirus industry has spent two decades formalising
scanner-as-product. The product-family map (signature DB / FP
submission portal / allowlist DB / on-access guard / boot-time
scan / HIPS / cloud lookup / provenance), the append-only FP/FN
ledger discipline, the quarterly re-review cadence, the per-
detector versioning, and the corpus-as-ground-truth practice
are well-evidenced in
industry~\cite{AntivirusTaxonomy:2010s:industry-practice}.
PrimitiveHub adopts this map row-for-row (ADR-0030 \S 3).

What carries over is methodology: paired FP and FN ledgers,
four-path triage classification (edit content / narrow allow +
ledger / detector ratchet / escalate), corpus as the regression
contract, F\textsubscript{1} as the operational metric, quarterly
re-review of every ledger entry. What is new is the substrate:
agent-context primitives are markdown documents with semantic
content, not binary executables. The detection techniques
diverge accordingly --- regex pools with markdown-context
awareness, UTS \#39 skeleton comparison for vendor names,
codepoint-class density checks --- even when the surrounding
methodology stays AV-industry-standard.

\subsection{Unicode-based attacks}

PrimitiveHub's T7 detector uses the UTS \#39 skeleton method
for confusable identifier
detection~\cite{UTS39:2024:unicode-confusables}. The method is
standardised; its application to vendor-name impersonation in
agent-context content is the new operational target. The
phishing-domain literature has documented confusables-based
attack detection at internet scale for nearly a
decade~\cite{Confusables:2017:phishing-detection}; bringing the
technique into the primitive-registry space extends it to a
new defense surface where the same attacker tradecraft has
been observed in field reports.

The scope of the v0.3.1 detector --- 43 single-word vendors in
the curated list, with the generic skeleton method
automatically extending to every added vendor --- explicitly
defers the full confusables.txt + UAX \#31 identifier-domain
generalisation until the vendor list outgrows manual curation
(see Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\subsection{Measurement methodology}

PrimitiveHub's precision / recall / F\textsubscript{1}
measurements use the standard binary-classification convention
documented in
Powers~\cite{Powers:2011:evaluation-precision-recall}.
Macro-averaging across the ten detectors is unweighted (each
CCI category is a separate defense layer; sample frequency is
not a class-imbalance argument). The holdout discipline
follows the train/test split tradition industry has used since
the 1990s~\cite{HoldoutValidation:1995:tradition}, applied
per-detector with the 80/20 ratio documented in
ADR-0038~\cite{primitivehub:2026:adr-0038}.

The per-sample reproducibility surface ---
\verb|bench/cli/replay.py| --- has no direct analogue in the
classical AV literature, where signature-database internals
were typically proprietary. The closer analogue is the open
test-corpus pattern from compiler-conformance work (the Ada
ACATS suite, the Khronos OpenGL test pool), where labeled
inputs and per-test outcomes are public so independent
implementations can be measured comparably. PrimitiveHub's
corpus + replay tool sit in the same tradition: open inputs,
open scoring code, open per-sample verdicts.

The CVE taxonomy (cited in CCI-008 invalid-CVE
detection)~\cite{MITRE:CVE:1999:taxonomy} and the US Patent
issuance taxonomy (cited in CCI-008 invalid-patent
detection)~\cite{USPTO:1790:patent-system} are the authority
catalogs whose absence-of-an-identifier signals our detector
reads as ``fake citation.'' Both are external taxonomies the
detector consults as ground truth at runtime.
